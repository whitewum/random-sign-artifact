\section{Model and post-failure estimation bias}\label{sec:model}
This section introduces the standard IBLT and the ideal peeling model, defines the requirement of the two-round protocol on the conditional miss rate, characterizes the structure of failure at low load, and analyzes the mechanism by which the post-failure bias of the standard IBLT varies with the sign composition. Notation used throughout the paper is collected in Appendix~\ref{app:notations}.

\subsection{The standard IBLT and the ideal peeling model}
The two sides hold sets $A$ and $B$, respectively, and need to find the symmetric difference $A\triangle B$, whose size $d=|A\triangle B|$ is unknown in advance. The standard IBLT consists of $M$ cells, each storing three fields: count, keySum, and checksum. When inserting a key $x$, the cell hash selects $k$ distinct cells from $[M]$, and accumulates $1$, $x$, and $\mathrm{chk}(x)$ into the three fields of these cells, respectively. After each side builds its table and the two are subtracted cell by cell, a shared key falls into the same cells and writes the same value in both tables, so all three fields cancel; only the contributions of the $d$ differing keys remain in the difference table, with a key present only in $A$ carrying direction $s_i=+1$ and a key present only in $B$ carrying $s_i=-1$. Denote the numbers of keys in the two directions by $d_+$ and $d_-$; the ordered pair $(d_+,d_-)$ is called the \emph{sign composition}, and $b=d_+-d_-=|A|-|B|$ is the difference between the two set sizes. Throughout the paper we use two extreme sign compositions: when all differing keys come from the same side ($|b|=d$), it is called the \emph{one-sided difference}, and when the two sides have equal numbers of differences ($d_+=d_-$, $b=0$), it is called the \emph{balanced difference}. These two cases correspond to common synchronization scenarios. The one-sided difference arises from one-way synchronization, where only one side inserts or deletes entries and the other side stays unchanged. The balanced difference arises from two-way synchronization, where each side modifies a small number of entries and the insertions and deletions roughly cancel. Real loads usually lie in between, and the specific position depends on how much each side changed. Decoding uses the peeling process: repeatedly find a pure cell containing a single unrecovered differing key, read out this key by multiplying keySum by the count, verify it with the checksum, and remove its contribution from all $k$ cells it belongs to; the removal may turn other cells pure, and the iteration continues until the table has no pure cell.

Under ideal purity (a cell with count $\pm1$ indeed contains a single unrecovered differing key), whether peeling succeeds is determined entirely by the mapping structure of differing keys to cells. Viewing the $M$ cells as vertices and the $k$ cells selected by each differing key as a hyperedge, the difference table corresponds to a random $k$-uniform hypergraph: the $d$ differing keys independently and uniformly choose $k$-subsets $S_i\subseteq[M]$, and different keys may choose the same subset. Denote by $\mathcal G$ the unsigned mapping with key labels, and by $H_{ij}=|S_i\cap S_j|$ the overlap of two edges, the number of cells jointly selected by differing keys $i$ and $j$. On $\mathcal G$, a pure cell is exactly an unsigned degree-one vertex, and peeling is equivalent to repeatedly deleting edges incident to degree-one vertices on the hypergraph. The deletion order does not affect the terminal state, and peeling always stops at the 2-core of the hypergraph; when the 2-core is nonempty, a decoding failure occurs, denoted as event $F$, whose probability $\Pr(F)$ is called the failure probability. Hence $F$ depends only on the unsigned mapping $\mathcal G$. When the load $d/M$ is below the critical value $\alpha_c$ (about $0.818$ for $k=3$ and about $0.772$ for $k=4$\cite{molloy,dw}), peeling succeeds with high probability, and the rare failure is determined by small cores such as duplicate edge pairs, decaying polynomially in $M$; in the critical window of width of order $M^{-1/2}$ around $\alpha_c$, the failure probability rises from near zero to near one\cite{dm}; past $\alpha_c$, peeling fails almost surely.

\subsection{The two-round protocol and the conditional miss rate}
Because the capacity $M$ must be fixed before $d$ is known, the two-round protocol\cite{wu2026selfsizing} first sends a small table of fixed size $M_1$: if the difference is small, peeling succeeds directly in the first round; if the first round suffers failure, the receiver reads an upper bound $U$ on $d$ from the count array of the first-round difference table, chooses the second-round size $m(U)$ according to $U$, and re-encodes and decodes with a fresh independent cell hash.

Denote by $C_v$ the count of cell $v$ in the difference table. Following \cite{wu2026selfsizing}, define the estimator $\widehat d$ of $d$ from the count sum $\sum_vC_v$ and the sum of squares $\sum_vC_v^2$ saved before peeling begins:
\begin{equation}
\widehat d=\frac{1}{k(1-k/M)}\biggl(\sum_vC_v^2-\frac1M\Bigl(\sum_vC_v\Bigr)^2\biggr),\label{eq:dhat}
\end{equation}
and denote by $s(n)$ its unconditional standard-deviation scale:
\begin{equation}
s(n)=\sqrt{\frac{2n(n-1)}{M-1}}.\label{eq:s}
\end{equation}
Under the standard IBLT, $\widehat d$ is exactly unbiased for $d$, has variance $s(d)^2$, and this holds for any sign composition; the expectation here is taken over all runs and is not conditioned on failure $F$, so it is called unconditional. If the upper bound $U$ were evaluated identically over all runs, adding a few standard deviations to $\widehat d$ would control the underestimate probability. But in the two-round protocol, when the first round succeeds the protocol ends immediately and $U$ need not be computed; only when the first round suffers a decoding failure (event $F$) does the receiver compute $U$ to decide the second-round table size. Once $U<d$ occurs, the second-round table's capacity falls below the preset margin and faces the risk of failing again. We call the underestimate event $U<d$ a \emph{miss}, and the underestimate probability given that the first round suffered failure, $\Pr(U<d\mid F)$, the \emph{conditional miss rate}. Hence the sizing rule must control the conditional miss rate:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(U<d\mid F)\le u,\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the target risk, which in practical deployment can be $0.01$ or $0.05$, i.e. a 1 \% or 5 \% underestimate risk.

From the perspective of the entire two-round protocol, a synchronization only misses when the first round suffers failure and the upper bound underestimates $d$, whose probability is the \emph{joint miss rate} $\Pr(F,\,U<d)=\Pr(F)\Pr(U<d\mid F)$. Keeping the conditional miss rate within $u$ guarantees that whether the first round lies in the overload region where failure is almost certain or in the low-load region where failure occurs occasionally, the joint miss rate never exceeds $u\Pr(F)$; on the failure samples without a miss ($U\ge d$), the second-round table has a sufficient capacity margin and hence completes decoding with high probability. Section~\ref{sec:protocol} gives the complete decomposition of this two-round total failure rate.

\subsection{Failure at low load and the planted distribution}\label{sec:plant}
\eqref{eq:target} is required to hold for all loads, and the low-load region needs separate treatment. When $d$ is much smaller than $M$, the failure probability decays polynomially in $M$, and failure is a rare event: the distribution conditioned on $F$ is hard to analyze directly, and direct simulation must generate many mappings to obtain one failure sample. The failure structure in this region, however, is simple. Call two edges with the same mapping a duplicate edge pair. A single edge cannot form a 2-core, and two edges form a 2-core if and only if they are a duplicate edge pair; when $d=o(M)$, the 2-cores formed by three or more edges are negligible relative to duplicate edge pairs. Hence failure is nearly the same event as the existence of a duplicate edge pair, and conditioning on $F$ is close to planting a pair of duplicate edges into a random mapping. This motivates the \emph{planted distribution}: choose a pair of keys uniformly, make them select the same uniformly random $k$-subset, and let the mappings of the remaining keys be independent and uniform.

\begin{proposition}[Failure is determined by duplicate edge pairs when $d=o(M)$]\label{prop:plant}
Fix $k\ge3$ and let $2\le d=o(M)$. Then the failure probability is asymptotically equal to the expected number of duplicate edge pairs, namely $\Pr(F)\sim\binom d2/\binom Mk$; and the total variation distance between the distribution of $\mathcal G$ conditioned on $F$ and the planted distribution tends to zero.
\end{proposition}

Duplicate edge pairs are the main source of IBLT failure; this mechanism was pointed out by Goodrich and Mitzenmacher\cite{gm}, and the finite-length analysis of Yugawa and Wadayama\cite{yw} also shows that stopping sets of size 2 dominate. Proposition~\ref{prop:plant} advances it to a total-variation approximation of the whole mapping, valid for all $d=o(M)$; the quantitative error bound and proof are in Lemma~\ref{lem:plant} of Appendix~\ref{app:graph}. Proposition~\ref{prop:plant} is useful both theoretically and experimentally. Theoretically, it reduces the problem conditioned on failure to a problem under the planted distribution, which carries no failure condition and is easy to compute directly; the limit theorems of Sections~\ref{sec:measurement} and \ref{sec:inversion} handle the failure condition through it when $d=o(M)$, and the risk decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} also uses it to estimate the first-round failure probability. Experimentally, it allows sampling directly from the planted distribution, every sample of which produces a failure; Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup} uses this planted sampling to evaluate low-load configurations that direct sampling cannot cover, and checks its finite-size accuracy on configurations where both samplings are feasible.

\subsection{Post-failure bias of the standard IBLT}
The difficulty in controlling \eqref{eq:target} is that the failure event $F$ selects the mapping $\mathcal G$. Failure samples concentrate on mappings with large overlap $H_{ij}$, i.e. different differing keys jointly select many cells: at low load they are mainly mappings containing duplicate edge pairs (Proposition~\ref{prop:plant}), and in the critical window and at higher load they are large cores with dense overlap. Consequently, any statistic depending on $H_{ij}$ deviates from its unconditional distribution once conditioned on $F$.

In the standard IBLT, the difference-table count is $C_v=\sum_{i:v\in S_i}s_i$, and the coding coefficient of each differing key is its fixed set direction $s_i\in\{+1,-1\}$. When summing $C_v^2$, the sum of the diagonal terms of individual keys is fixed at $kd$, and the estimation error comes entirely from the cross terms $s_is_jH_{ij}$ produced by different keys on shared cells. The excess overlap of same-sign pairs raises $\widehat d$, and that of opposite-sign pairs lowers $\widehat d$. Hence the conditional relative bias of $\widehat d$ decomposes exactly into the product of two factors.
\begin{proposition}[Bias decomposition of the standard IBLT]\label{prop:projection}
In the standard IBLT, for any sign composition $(d_+,d_-)$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\E[\widehat d\mid F]}{d}-1=c_F\Bigl(\frac{b^2}{d}-1\Bigr),\qquad c_F=\frac{\E[H_{12}\mid F]-k^2/M}{k(1-k/M)}.\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:std}. Because $F$ is invariant under permutation of the key labels, $\E[H_{ij}\mid F]$ is the same for all $i\ne j$, and the formula takes the pair of differing keys $1$ and $2$ as a representative. $c_F$ depends only on the selection of the mapping by failure and measures how much the inter-edge overlap in the failure sample exceeds its unconditional mean $k^2/M$; $b^2/d-1$ depends only on the sign composition $(d_+,d_-)$. Under the one-sided difference ($|b|=d$), the sign product of every pair of intersecting edges is $+1$, so $b^2/d-1=d-1$, and the excess overlap adds in the same direction and is amplified fully onto $\widehat d$; under the balanced difference ($b\approx0$), same-sign and opposite-sign pairs are comparable in number, the positive and negative cross terms cancel, and only a negligible $-c_F$ bias remains.

Each color corresponds to one level of failure probability; all configurations lie in the critical window, with $d/M$ in 0.812--0.822 for $k=3$ and 0.633--0.639 for $k=6$. \end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:bias} shows this decomposition at $M=16384$ with five levels of failure probability (0.10 to 0.90) each for $k=3$ and $k=6$; the five sign compositions on the same configuration share the same batch of mappings, so peeling succeeds or fails identically. Since $b^2/d-1=d(b/d)^2-1$, the relative bias at each level is a linear function of $(b/d)^2$ with slope determined by $c_F$. After determining $c_F$ from the one-sided point, the predictions of \eqref{eq:projection} for the remaining levels differ from the measured values by no more than twice the standard error of the difference. For $k=3$ at failure probability 0.10, the relative bias of the one-sided difference is $+0.70\%$, about 0.64 times the standard deviation $s(d)$; the higher the failure probability, the closer the failure sample is to the full sample and the weaker the selection, so $|c_F|$ decreases. The sign of $c_F$ also changes with $k$: for $k=6$, $c_F<0$, and the one-sided failure sample is instead biased low, with relative bias $-0.43\%$ at failure probability 0.10 (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}(b)). The sign of the bias changes with $k$, and its magnitude varies with the sign composition between zero and the one-sided value, so the receiver cannot offset it with a fixed correction (Figure~\ref{fig:composition}).

In real synchronization, the receiver does not know $(d_+,d_-)$ in advance, and from the count array of the difference table alone it cannot distinguish whether a larger observed $\widehat d$ comes from a larger true $d$ or from the failure-induced bias under a one-sided difference; the upper-bound rule of \cite{wu2026selfsizing} therefore needs simulation calibration by load interval outside the deep overload region. From \eqref{eq:projection} one sees that the bias arises because the standard IBLT directly treats the set direction $s_i$ as the accumulation sign of count, and the sign composition determines the factor by which $c_F$ is amplified; once the positive and negative signs are balanced, this factor approaches $-1$, and the offset brought by failure selection vanishes. This suggests a natural idea: if the set difference of any sign composition could carry balanced random signs in the difference table while peeling still recovers the original set direction of each differing key, then the post-failure estimator would no longer be affected by the sign composition. Section~\ref{sec:design} proposes the random-sign IBLT accordingly.
