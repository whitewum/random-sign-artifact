\section{Experimental evaluation}\label{sec:exp}
We address three experimental questions. First, we test whether random-sign encoding removes the dependence of the post-failure estimate on sign composition while preserving decoding (Section~\ref{sec:exp-decode}). Second, we examine the shape of the post-failure statistic's lower tail (Section~\ref{sec:exp-dist}) and how it determines the conditional miss rate and extra capacity of the two rules at finite size (Section~\ref{sec:exp-cover}). Third, we measure reliability and communication cost when the upper bound is used in the two-round protocol on a complete IBLT (Section~\ref{sec:exp-e2e}), along with computational cost (Section~\ref{sec:exp-cost}). Section~\ref{sec:exp-deploy} summarizes the guarantees and costs of each rule choice and gives deployment recommendations.

We conduct statistical experiments (\exptag{STAT}) and protocol experiments (\exptag{PROTO}). The statistical experiments study the distribution of the post-failure statistic, using the configurations in Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup}. The protocol experiments run the two-round protocol on a complete IBLT, using the configurations in Section~\ref{sec:exp-e2e}. A few figures use a single additional configuration to illustrate the distribution's shape, with parameters specified in the caption.

\subsection{Configurations and sampling for the statistical experiments}\label{sec:exp-setup}
The statistical experiments use a sampler that independently generates $d$ uniform random hyperedges and runs peeling, without constructing a complete IBLT. On each mapping, we compute $X=(\widehat d-d)/s(d)$ for the standard encoding under one-sided and balanced differences, together with the exact integers $\sum_vC_v$ and $\sum_vC_v^2$ for the random-sign encoding. We then compute both rules' upper bounds from the same observation.

Each configuration is specified by $(k,M,d)$. We describe its position along two axes: $c=d/\sqrt M$ (\eqref{eq:c}) and the first-round load $d/(\alpha_cM)$. These axes control different effects. The parameter $c$ determines the statistical scale of the estimation error. For small $c$, the post-failure $\widehat d-d$ is dominated by the $\pm2$ offsets of a few duplicate edge pairs. For large $c$, it approaches a continuous distribution. The load determines the position relative to the peeling phase transition: subcritical below 0.95, in the critical window from 0.95 to 1.05, and overloaded above 1.05. For fixed $k,M$, the axes satisfy $d/(\alpha_cM)=c/(\alpha_c\sqrt M)$. Thus, changing $M$ is necessary to vary the load while keeping $c$ fixed and separating the two effects.

The statistical experiments use $k=3,4,5$ and $M\in\{256,1024,4096,16384\}$. For each pair $(k,M)$, $d$ ranges over the union of three sets: the 8 values corresponding to $c\in\{0.5,1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}$, the 5 values corresponding to load $\in\{0.95,1,1.05,1.5,3\}$, and the small differences $d=2,3,6$. Excluding loads above 3 leaves 189 configurations, with about 20,000 failures collected for each. Applying the same rule at $M=64$ gives 42 configurations, which we use separately to test the rules' limits of applicability. Figure~\ref{fig:exp-grid} in the appendix shows the configurations on both axes.

\paragraph{Planted sampling.}
We obtain failure samples by direct sampling or planted sampling, depending on the load. Near or above the critical load, failure is common. We generate random mappings and retain failures, obtaining samples from the true failure-conditioned distribution. At low load, the failure probability is small and direct sampling is too costly. By Proposition~\ref{prop:plant}, when $d=o(M)$, failure is almost always caused by a pair of keys with identical mappings. We can construct such samples directly: choose a random pair of difference keys to share the same set of $k$ cells, then draw the mappings and signs of the remaining keys independently. We use direct sampling for the 84 statistical configurations with high failure rates and planted sampling for the remaining 105. The figures distinguish the two sampling methods (Appendix~\ref{app:sampling-impl}).

The planted-sampling control (\exptag{PLANT}) compares the two sample types on 12 planted configurations where direct sampling remains affordable. Among directly sampled failures, the fraction caused by a duplicate edge pair ranges from 0.988 to 1.000. The KS distance for $X$ ranges from 0.0048 to 0.0079, comparable to sampling error at this sample size, and the Skellam rule's miss/u values also agree. The planted approximation deviates only near the critical load at small $M$. The planted configurations in the statistical experiments lie outside this region (Appendix~\ref{app:planted}).

\subsection{Random-sign encoding removes dependence on sign composition}\label{sec:exp-decode}
We verify two properties of random-sign encoding: the post-failure estimate is independent of sign composition, and decoding gives exactly the same result as the standard encoding.

\paragraph{Conditional bias.}
Under random-sign encoding, the post-failure $\widehat d$ is unbiased for every sign composition. Under the standard encoding, conditioning the mapping on failure introduces a bias in $\widehat d$ that depends on sign composition. Figure~\ref{fig:encoding} compares three conditions on the same batch of 4 million mappings, with identical peeling outcomes in each trial. For the standard encoding, the distribution conditional on failure shifts right by about $0.6s(d)$ under a one-sided difference and is almost unbiased under a balanced difference. For random-sign encoding, the distribution conditional on failure almost coincides with the distribution over all samples (numerical values in Appendix~\ref{app:dist}).

The standard encoding's bias depends on both sign composition and $k$, so correcting it requires information unavailable to the receiver in advance. Figure~\ref{fig:composition}(a) compares the encodings at five sign compositions. The solid lines for the standard encoding are near zero at the balanced point and deviate further as the difference becomes more one-sided. The direction depends on $k$: for a one-sided difference, the bias is $+0.638$ at $k=3$ and $-0.387$ at $k=6$ (mechanism in Appendix~\ref{app:std}). The receiver knows $k$ and hence the direction of the bias. Determining the correction's magnitude also requires $(d_+,d_-)$, which is unknown in advance. The dashed lines for random-sign encoding remain near zero. Even with the vertical axis magnified about 40 times (Figure~\ref{fig:composition}(b)), the five sign compositions show no discernible difference. This agrees with Proposition~\ref{prop:composition}: the coding coefficients $s_i\varepsilon_i$ are iid random signs with a distribution independent of $(d_+,d_-)$. The 84 direct-sampling configurations in the statistical experiments give the same conclusion (Appendix~\ref{app:dist}).

\paragraph{Decoding equivalence.}
Random-sign encoding preserves the set of keys recovered by decoding (Proposition~\ref{prop:decode}) and therefore inherits the standard IBLT's threshold and failure probability. The decoding-equivalence test (\exptag{DEC}) verifies this in paired trials on a complete IBLT. Both encodings share the same keys and cell hash. Each trial checks the first-round outcome, every accepted key, and the final recovered set against an independent degree-one peeling reference. We observe zero disagreements in more than 500 million paired trials (Appendix~\ref{app:decode-exp}). Table~\ref{tab:decode} reports failure probabilities near the threshold. After normalizing the load by the respective $\alpha_c$, the curves for the five values of $k$ almost coincide. The transition band narrows as $M$ grows, consistent with the critical window of width of order $M^{-1/2}$ in Section~\ref{sec:model}.

Random-sign encoding places the same additional demands on the decoder as a balanced difference does in the standard IBLT. Random signs allow cells of degree greater than one to have count $\pm1$ (Section~\ref{sec:design}). All such candidates are rejected at the checksum step, with zero false acceptances. Their frequency under random-sign encoding is independent of sign composition and equals that under standard encoding with a balanced difference (Figure~\ref{fig:decode} in the appendix). Both encodings use the same cell fields and byte widths. Random-sign encoding additionally sends the set size $|A|$ (8 bytes) with the table to recover the lower bound $|b|$.

\subsection{The discrete limit distribution accurately describes the post-failure lower tail at finite size}\label{sec:exp-dist}
The limit distribution in Section~\ref{sec:inversion} almost coincides with the empirical post-failure lower tail without fitting. Figure~\ref{fig:crosscdf} compares six configurations with $c$ ranging from 0.25 to 8 at $M=16384$. It shows the empirical CDF of post-failure $(\widetilde d-d)/s(d)$, the limit distribution of Theorem~\ref{thm:cross}, and $N(0,1)$. The empirical CDF almost coincides with the limit distribution in all six configurations, whereas $N(0,1)$ deviates clearly at small $c$. We compute the empirical distribution exactly from integer sums of squares and use $G_d$ from the main text for the limit distribution, with no fitted parameters. Finite-size error decreases as $M$ grows. At $c=1,2,4$, the KS distance falls from at most 0.034 at $M=256$ to at most 0.012 at $M=16384$ (Table~\ref{tab:limit-ks} in the appendix).

\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../figure/fig-crossover-cdf.pdf}
\caption{Post-failure lower tail of $(\widetilde d-d)/s(d)$ at different $c$ ($k=3$, $M=16384$; 400,000 planted samples per configuration for $c=1,2,4$, and 100,000 for the rest). The limit distribution is computed directly from Theorem~\ref{thm:cross} without fitting. Below each title are the KS distances from the empirical CDF to the limit distribution and to $N(0,1)$. Gray lines mark 1\% and 5\%.}
\label{fig:crosscdf}
\end{figure}

Duplicate edge pairs create the steps in the lower tail, which fill in as $c$ grows. At small $c$, failure is almost always caused by a single duplicate edge pair. If the two edges have opposite signs, the observation is 2 below $d$. This $-2$ offset is large relative to $s(d)$. At $c=0.25$, it corresponds to a standardized error of about $-5.7$, and about four tenths of the failure samples fall on this step, visible as the large step at $c=0.25$ in Figure~\ref{fig:crosscdf}. As $c$ grows, overlap among the remaining edges contributes many $\pm2/k$ offsets that fill in the steps. The lower tail gradually approaches the Gaussian (Appendix~\ref{app:limit}).

This discrete structure determines the two rules' finite-size behavior. The Gaussian rule treats the discrete left tail as continuous. At small $c$, $s(d)$ is small, so the $-2$ offset from opposite signs in the duplicate edge pair lies far in the Gaussian tail. The probability of rejecting the true $d$ then exceeds $u$. The Skellam rule uses $G_n$, the equally weighted mixture CDF for the equal-sign and opposite-sign cases of the duplicate edge pair. It includes the probability of the observed value itself and accepts a candidate when $G_n(\widetilde d)>u$. When $u$ lies strictly inside the CDF jump at a lattice point, that point is accepted, making the rejection probability under the null distribution strictly smaller than $u$; in general, it is at most $u$. This explains the rule's conservatism in the small-$c$ and low-load regions, where the possible values are sparse. The conditional miss rates in the next subsection agree with both observations.

\subsection{Conditional miss rate and extra capacity at finite size}\label{sec:exp-cover}
We test both rules' risk at finite table sizes and measure the extra capacity required by the upper bound. Theorem~\ref{thm:coverage} guarantees that the Skellam rule's conditional miss rate is asymptotically at most $u$ for all $d\ge2$. The Gaussian rule has the same guarantee only when $c\to\infty$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:gauss}). The conditional miss rate is the fraction of failure samples with a miss ($U<d$), reported as miss/$u$. Values at most 1 indicate risk within the target. Extra capacity is $(U-d)/d$.

\paragraph{Conditional miss rate.}
The Skellam rule keeps risk near the target throughout the region. The circles in Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} lie near miss/u $=1$ across the 189 configurations. The maximum, 1.04, is within sampling error for 20,000 failures per configuration. We retest the 12 configurations closest to the risk upper bound with independent samples (\exptag{WORST}), covering bounded $c$, the critical window, and overload. In Figure~\ref{fig:confirm}, all estimated miss/u values are at most 0.99. The largest upper confidence bound is $1.024u$, meeting the $1.1u$ criterion set before sampling (Appendix~\ref{app:cover-more}). Low-load configurations with $c<8$ are conservative, especially at $u=0.01$. As explained in Section~\ref{sec:exp-dist}, the lower tail is concentrated on a few lattice points, so the rule cannot exhaust the risk budget exactly between them.

The Gaussian rule meets the target only at large $c$. The triangles in Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} lie near 1 when $c\ge8$ and rise rapidly as $c$ decreases. The miss rate is already more than 8 times the target at $u=0.01$, $c=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:gauss-c} in the appendix). This excess persists as table size grows, consistent with the finite limit in Theorem~\ref{thm:cross}. The discrete structure again explains the behavior. When $c$ is sufficiently small, the post-failure observation falls near $d+2$ and $d-2$ with probability one half each (Proposition~\ref{prop:smalld}). The Gaussian tail assigns the latter an extremely small probability, so the rule accepts the candidate $d-2$ and the miss rate tends to 1/2.

\paragraph{Extra capacity.}
Extra capacity depends on table size and decreases as $M^{-1/2}$, with nearly identical values for both rules. At $u=0.01$ and $c\ge8$, the Skellam rule's median extra capacity drops from 25\% at $M=256$ to 2.6\% at $M=16384$. This follows because $U-d$ is about 2.3--2.8 times $s(d)$ and $s(d)/d\approx\sqrt{2/M}$. In the two-round protocol, this extra capacity appears as the increase in second-round table size over the oracle. In Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}(a), it follows the dotted line with slope $-1/2$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp-e2e}). When $c$ is bounded, the difference itself is small and few extra elements are needed. At $c=4$, the median $U-d$ is 15 (Appendix~\ref{app:cover-more}).

We recommend $M\ge1024$ for deployment. The null distribution in Section~\ref{sec:inversion} omits overlap between the duplicate edge pair and the remaining edges. Its occurrence rate, $(d-2)k^2/M$, grows as table size decreases. At $M=64$, this omission becomes nonnegligible: the Skellam rule's maximum miss/u is 1.08, beyond sampling error. At $M=256$, we observe no excess in miss/u beyond sampling error. However, the omitted structure occurs 4 times as often as at $M=1024$, and median extra capacity reaches 25\%.

\subsection{The two-round protocol on a complete IBLT}\label{sec:exp-e2e}
Theorem~\ref{thm:protocol} decomposes the two-round failure probability into three terms: the joint miss rate, peeling failure with sufficient capacity, and checksum error. We run the two-round protocol on a complete IBLT to compare the first two terms in practice, measure the random-sign IBLT's communication cost, and compare it with the existing calibration rule.

The protocol experiments (\exptag{PROTO}) use $k=3,4,5$, first-round table sizes $M_1\in\{256,1024,4096,16384\}$, and first-round loads 0.95, 1, 1.05, 1.5, 3. We test two sign compositions, one-sided (all on the $A$ side) and balanced, for a total of 120 configurations. The two sides also hold 1000 common elements. We fix each configuration's trial count in advance using a pilot estimate of the first-round failure probability. We target about 20,000 first-round failures, capped at 3,000,000 trials. Configurations with no first-round failure in the pilot receive a fixed 100,000 trials. Both values of $u$ use the same trials. After each first-round failure, we run the second round separately with table sizes determined by three upper bounds: the Skellam rule, the Gaussian rule, and an oracle using the true $d$. Both rules use $U_b=\max(U,|b|)$ as in Section~\ref{sec:flow}. The second-round table size is $\max\{\lceil U_b/(0.8\alpha_c)\rceil,64\}$, with a fresh cell hash. All three upper bounds share the keys and hashes within each trial, allowing paired comparisons that differ only in the upper bound (implementation details in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

The two-round protocol achieves reliability comparable to the oracle. In Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}(b), both rules' final failure rates fall in the same range as the oracle's, all at most 0.13\% when $M_1\ge1024$. At $u=0.01$, their maximum is at most the oracle's (Table~\ref{tab:e2e} in the appendix). The two rules are statistically indistinguishable. Final failures arise mainly from second-round peeling, consistent with the expected dominance of the second term in Theorem~\ref{thm:protocol}. At $u=0.01$, at most 6\% of final failures follow a miss. The second-round table size includes a margin of $1/(0.8\alpha_c)$, which absorbs most slight underestimates. This margin also explains why the rules perform at least as well as the oracle: their upper bounds usually exceed $d$, yielding slightly larger second-round tables and fewer peeling failures.

Extra communication cost depends only on $M_1$ and decreases as $M_1^{-1/2}$. In Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}(a), configurations with different $k$, loads, and sign compositions almost coincide at each $M_1$ and follow the dotted line with slope $-1/2$. At $u=0.01$, the Skellam rule's increase in second-round table size over the oracle drops from 25.6\% at $M_1=256$ to 2.6\% at $M_1=16384$, consistent with the extra capacity in Section~\ref{sec:exp-cover}. In total bytes, the overhead drops from 14.7\% to 1.5\%.

\paragraph{Comparison with the existing calibration rule.}
Under standard encoding, the existing calibration rule depends on sign composition, reflecting the bias in Section~\ref{sec:exp-decode}. We use the calibration factor $q_{0.01}$ from \cite{wu2026selfsizing} in the same protocol, setting $U=\lceil\widehat d/q_{0.01}\rceil$ and keeping all other settings identical to those for our two rules (Table~\ref{tab:vldb} in the appendix). Under standard encoding at loads 0.95--1.0, the same factor gives conditional miss rates of $0.05$--$0.40u$ for a one-sided difference and $0.86$--$0.94u$ for a balanced difference, an order-of-magnitude difference. Random-sign encoding brings both into $0.80$--$1.01u$. The factor requires separate simulation calibration for each parameter configuration. The published values cover only $k=3$, $M_1\le4096$, and $u=0.01$. For these parameters, the calibration rule and the Skellam rule have similar final failure rates and total byte costs. We compute the Skellam rule directly from the theoretical distribution in Section~\ref{sec:inversion}, without calibration for any $k$, $M$, load, or $u$.

\subsection{Computational cost}\label{sec:exp-cost}
Almost all computation beyond standard encoding comes from sizing with the Skellam rule. The overhead of random-sign encoding itself is negligible. Relative to the Gaussian rule, the Skellam rule increases total protocol time by less than half at loads up to 1.5. At load 3, sizing dominates and total time is 2.8--3.8 times that of the Gaussian rule. Combining the two rules based on the first-round observation avoids this cost (Section~\ref{sec:switch}).

With random-sign encoding, the decoder processes every difference as a balanced difference. For a one-sided difference, the number of candidates checked per recovered key rises from 1.00 to 1.77 ($k=3$). The resulting increase in decoding time is less than 1\% of total protocol time.

The Skellam rule's sizing cost grows with load. Computing its exact CDF requires constructing and querying Poisson probability tables. These depend only on $(M,k)$ and the candidate $n$, so a receiver can cache and reuse them across synchronizations. Table~\ref{tab:proto} reports total protocol time at $M_1=16384$, including first-round encoding and decoding, sizing, and the second round. At loads 1.0--1.05, the Skellam rule takes 14--23\% longer than the Gaussian rule. At load 1.5, it takes 39--43\% longer. Sizing cost grows roughly as $c^2$, and at load 3 the Skellam rule's total time is 57--88 ms. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} reports the conditions for the computational-cost measurement (\exptag{COST}), time per sizing operation under different cache conditions, and memory use.

\subsection{Rule choice and deployment}\label{sec:exp-deploy}
For deployment, we recommend combining the two rules based on the first-round observation (Section~\ref{sec:switch}). Table~\ref{tab:sizing-options} summarizes the guarantees and costs of each rule and their combination. The Gaussian rule requires only a microsecond-scale scalar search. Its guarantee applies when $d\gg\sqrt M$, making it suitable for applications whose workloads ensure this condition and prioritize low latency. Table size alone leaves open cases with bounded $c$ or fixed small $d$. The Skellam rule applies to all $d\ge2$ without knowing which load region contains $d$. It requires millisecond-scale computation using Poisson tables, which dominates protocol time when the first round is heavily overloaded (Section~\ref{sec:exp-cost}). The combination uses the Gaussian rule at high load and the Skellam rule otherwise. It retains the uniform guarantee and matches the Gaussian rule's runtime in the overload region.
