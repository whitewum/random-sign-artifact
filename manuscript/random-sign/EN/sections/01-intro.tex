\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
In many core scenarios of distributed systems, two ends of a network each hold a set $A$ and $B$ and need to find their symmetric difference $A\triangle B$, the elements that exist on only one side. This problem is called set reconciliation. The Invertible Bloom Lookup Table (IBLT)\cite{gm,eppstein2011whatsdifference} is the standard data structure for this task: each side builds a table of $M$ cells, and each element is written through hashing into the count, keySum, and checksum fields of $k$ cells; after the receiver subtracts the two tables cell by cell, elements shared by both sides write the same value to the same group of cells and cancel exactly, leaving only the contributions of the $d=|A\triangle B|$ differing elements in the difference table. An element present only in $A$ has direction $+1$, and an element present only in $B$ has direction $-1$. When the difference size $d$ is not too large relative to the table size $M$ (the load $d/M$ is below the peeling threshold), the receiver can repeatedly find a pure cell containing a single differing element and strip away all differing elements one by one through the peeling process; once $d$ exceeds the table's capacity, peeling stalls partway and a decoding failure occurs.

Using an IBLT raises a basic tension: the table size $M$ must be fixed when the table is built, while the difference size $d$ is precisely the quantity unknown before synchronization completes. In real systems, $d$ for the same pair of nodes can vary by several orders of magnitude across time: during routine synchronization the two sides are almost identical and $d$ is small; after network partition recovery or a batch write, $d$ can grow sharply. If a large table is always allocated for the worst case, most routine synchronizations waste bandwidth; if a small table is allocated, a large difference causes a decoding failure. The two-round protocol\cite{wu2026selfsizing} provides a natural way out: the first round sends a fixed small table, so that a routine small difference completes directly within one round; when the first round suffers a decoding failure because the difference is too large, the difference table is not actually wasted, and its count array still retains the traces left by all $d$ differing elements superimposed. The receiver only needs to estimate an upper bound $U$ on $d$ from the sum of squared counts of the first-round difference table, and the second round allocates a table of sufficient capacity according to $U$, completing the synchronization within two rounds. The key to this mechanism is controlling the underestimate risk under failure (the conditional miss rate): once $U<d$ (a miss occurs), the second-round table faces the danger of failure again because its capacity is insufficient; conversely, if $U$ is over-amplified for safety, the second-round transmission bandwidth is wasted.

The work of \cite{wu2026selfsizing} proposed an analysis method that uses the count sum and the sum of squared counts of the first-round difference table to construct an unbiased estimator $\widehat d$ and invert an upper bound $U$. However, when estimating $d$ from a difference table that suffered failure, the real difficulty is that the decoding failure itself selects the sample, and this selection couples with the sign composition of the set difference (the ratio of the number of differing elements from $A$ to those from $B$). When estimating $d$ with the sum of squared counts of that method, each element's own contribution is fixed, and the estimation error comes entirely from the cross terms produced when different differing elements fall into the same cell. In the standard IBLT, the difference table directly uses each element's set-membership direction ($+1$ or $-1$) as the accumulation sign of count: when two same-direction elements fall into the same cell, their counts add in the same direction and the sum of squares increases; when two opposite-direction elements fall into the same cell, they cancel. At the same time, peeling fails precisely because the position mapping of differing elements to cells has too much overlap. These two factors combine to produce a systematic bias in the failure sample. Take the one-sided difference (all differing elements come from the same side, common in one-way data synchronization such as a replica missing only the data newly written to the primary) as an example: all cross terms have the same sign, and the high overlap selected by failure is amplified in the same direction into the sum of squared counts, making the estimate deviate markedly; in two-way synchronization where each side writes new data, the two sides' differences are comparable, called the balanced difference, in which case the positive and negative cross terms largely cancel and the post-failure estimated mean is almost unchanged. More troublesome is that the magnitude of this bias varies continuously with the two sides' difference ratio, and its sign reverses with the number of hashes $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:model}). Because the receiver at decoding failure does not know whether the unknown difference comes entirely from one side or from both, faced with the observed sum of squared counts it cannot tell how much comes from the true $d$ and how much from the direction bias brought by failure selection. Therefore, when constructing an upper bound on the standard IBLT, \cite{wu2026selfsizing} has to rely on offline simulation calibration by configuration, except in the deep overload region.

The core insight of this paper is: since the bias originates from the standard IBLT treating the set-membership direction directly as the in-table accumulation sign, decoupling the element's set direction from the in-table accumulation sign removes this coupling at its source. Accordingly, this paper proposes the random-sign IBLT. The approach is lightweight: the two sides share in advance a random-sign hash independent of the position mapping, assigning each key a random $+1$ or $-1$; when inserting an element, the element's random sign is multiplied uniformly into the count, keySum, and checksum fields.

This modification satisfies the requirements of both decoding and estimation. On the decoding side, elements shared by the two sides are multiplied by the same random sign in both tables and still cancel exactly after subtraction. When a cell contains only one unrecovered differing element, its count is $+1$ or $-1$; multiplying keySum by the count cancels the random sign exactly (since $(\pm1)^2=1$) and reads out the element's key as is; after reading the key, computing its random sign and comparing it with the count recovers whether it originally belonged to set $A$ or $B$. Therefore, under ideal purity, the random-sign IBLT can strip exactly the same elements as the standard IBLT at every step, and the decoding threshold, the failure probability, and the number and byte width of the cell fields all remain unchanged. On the statistical estimation side, whether the true set difference is one-sided or balanced, the coding coefficient of each differing element in the difference table becomes an independent uniform random sign. More importantly, whether peeling succeeds is determined only by which cells the elements fall into (the mapping), while the cancellation of the estimation error is determined by the random signs; the two are fully independent. No matter how special a mapping the failure event selects, once that mapping is fixed, the random signs placed on it remain independent and uniform, cancelling out. Using random signs to cancel cross terms is a classical idea dating back to the AMS sketch and CountSketch\cite{alon1999spacecomplexity,charikar2004countsketch}; this paper implements it as a peelable data-structure design, giving a single table the ability of both difference recovery and difference-size estimation, and thereby enabling a rigorous statistical analysis of the post-failure estimator.

\paragraph{Main results and contributions.}
Building on the decoupling of encoding and sign, this paper first presents the encoding and peeling algorithm of the random-sign IBLT (Section~\ref{sec:design}), proving that under ideal purity its decoding behavior is identical to that of the standard IBLT, and that the joint distribution of the difference-table statistic and the mapping is the same for all sign compositions. We then prove that the estimator $\widehat d$ is exactly unbiased on every fixed mapping, so that its conditional mean after failure equals $d$, and fully characterize the conditional distribution of the estimation error over all load scales (Sections~\ref{sec:measurement} and \ref{sec:inversion}): at large difference scales we derive a Gaussian rule based on the conditional Gaussian limit; at small difference scales we characterize a discrete limit distribution, constructing a Skellam rule that requires no offline simulation calibration and uniformly controls the conditional miss rate for all difference sizes, together with an efficient planted sampling method for the rare failure at low load. The experiments (Section~\ref{sec:exp}) comprehensively verify that the random-sign encoding has the same decoding behavior as the standard encoding, show that the Skellam rule keeps the conditional miss rate strictly within the target risk over the entire load region spanning several orders of magnitude, reliably complete synchronization when attached to the two-round protocol, and achieve a total transmission cost close to the ideal lower bound of knowing the true $d$.
